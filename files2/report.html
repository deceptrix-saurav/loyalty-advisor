1. The problem and who has it

Millions of small businesses (cafes, kirana stores, salons, boutiques, online shops) are told to "start a loyalty program". App vendors say it, competitors do it, and every owner has heard that regular customers matter. The decision is almost always made by gut feel or by copying the shop next door.

How owners deal with it today: copy a neighbour's stamp card, sign up with an app vendor whose calculator assumes the program works, or ask a general chatbot that happily invents a figure. None of these checks the one question that decides the outcome: does the extra profit from changed behaviour beat everything given away in rewards? Most reward cost goes to customers who would have bought anyway.

What a wrong guess costs, from the app's own engine. An example cafe (3,000 bills a month, ₹250 average bill, 65% margin, 2.9% of member spend given back) gains ₹1,846 a month if members spend 6% more, but loses ₹1,059 a month if they spend only 2% more. That is ₹12,708 a year, quietly, with no one to point it out. These are engine outputs on illustrative inputs, not field measurements.

Where this idea comes from

I came to this from an earlier project: a strategy whitepaper on a real airport loyalty program (Aeroknots, at Delhi Airport, about 70 million passengers a year and 400+ shops). Its findings are exactly the failure modes a small owner faces, just at a larger scale:

  • Shops already paid 22 to 38% of sales in fees and revenue share, so margins were thin. The program added 1.1% (earn only) up to 10% (burn only) of the bill on top.
  • Much airport buying is impulsive, so sales rose only for planned purchases. The lift was small.
  • Nobody had settled who pays for the points, and customers were expected to redeem at a few "winner" shops while others funded them.
  • The vendor's financial model was found to lack the detail needed for an investment decision, yet commitments had been made.

A large operator can pay consultants to find this. A shop owner cannot. Loyalty Advisor is that check, made free, fast and understandable for the common man.

What is different, and why it matters

  • It starts from "should I?", not "how do I set it up?" Platforms assume you have decided. Most owners have not, and a "no, not yet" is the most valuable answer for a shop with few repeat customers.
  • It sizes the reward to your margin. The reward rate is the largest one that still pays in the expected case with headroom, never below a level customers would notice. If those two conflict, the verdict says so.
  • Maths in code, words from AI. Every figure is computed by tested code and the AI is only allowed to explain it. A guard rejects any number in AI text that is not in the verified facts.
  • It ends with a kill switch. Each plan lists red flags and stop rules, so a failing program is stopped at week 12, not year 2.

How they are organised

Work flows one way, left to right then back along the bottom row. Code does everything that must be right (maths, verdict, checks). AI agents do everything that needs language (reading messy text, choosing what to look at, explaining, personalising). The Analyst is the only agent that chooses its own steps, and it can only call read-only tools bound to the owner's profile.

Models

All agents use the course model gpt-6-luna through the course Portkey key. The tasks are extraction, tool selection and short plain-language writing; the hard reasoning lives in code. That is why one small, cheap model is enough, and why using one model for every agent keeps the prompt prefix identical so the provider's prompt cache can reuse it. Section 4 measures whether this holds up.

What I did to keep cost down

  • The maths is code, not AI. The model never reasons through numbers, so calls are short and the answers cannot drift.
  • Form-only runs skip the Intake call. If the owner fills in the boxes, no AI is needed to read text.
  • Saved answers. The same facts return the same Strategist and Designer output at zero tokens. Because the facts are identical, nothing is lost.
  • Lean mode skips the Analyst's model calls and runs the standard checks in code.
  • Cache-friendly prompts. A long identical rule block comes first in every call; per-business data comes last.
  • Short, structured outputs with length limits, and tools bound to the profile so the model never retypes numbers.
  • Hard caps on tokens per run, per client per day, and overall (see Safety).

7. Limits and next steps

What does not work yet

  • The lift is assumed, not measured. The 2%, 6% and 12% extra-spend scenarios and the enrolment rates are planning assumptions. The app says so everywhere, but they are the weakest link.
  • Defaults are generic. Margins and visit rates by business type are starting points the owner should override; each default is listed with its basis.
  • No memory between visits, and no import of real sales data. Six designs only (no coalition or partner programs). Four currencies.
  • Not validated with enough real owners yet.

What I would build in the next month

  • Pilot tracker: paste weekly sales for members and non-members and get the real lift, the real break-even gap and a scale/adjust/stop recommendation.
  • Calibrate the assumptions from pilot results across many shops.
  • WhatsApp front end and Hindi and other languages, since many owners work on a phone.
  • Supplier-funded and partner rewards (the airport lesson on who pays), and POS exports for one-click setup.
